Schismogenesis

Gregory Bateson's word for what happens when two sides keep reacting to each other until the reacting itself is what drives them apart. A couple where one nags and the other withdraws. Two neighbours outdoing each other's fences. Two parties answering every move with a slightly bigger one. The pattern is the same in all three, and nobody caught in it has to intend where it ends up.

Below is a small simulation of that. It takes a few minutes, and it starts with a question you will probably get wrong — which is the point.

Before you scroll: what would you say caused it?

These three runs start from the same population and were fitted to the same curve — nothing makes them coincide on their own. That is the point rather than a trick: the line is compatible with all three, so it cannot be the evidence for any of them. The field guide calls this equifinality, and warns that “the pattern is not the mechanism” (§13.1).

Situations to try

Each of these is a caricature — a mechanism drawn small enough to watch, not a measurement of anything.

Rivalry is symmetrical schismogenesis: each answers boasting with boasting, so the pair climbs together. Dominance is complementary: assertion draws out submission, which invites more assertion, so the pair forks. Bateson argued in Naven (1936) that either runs to breakdown unless something checks it.

Everything here is a caricature small enough to watch, and the political framing is a worked example rather than a claim about any country. Nothing in it is fitted to real data; section references point at the research field guide in this repository, which sets out what would be needed to make a claim of that kind and why this is not one.
